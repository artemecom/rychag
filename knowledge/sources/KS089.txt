Ofcom Sets Ground Rules for AI in Customer Service - A Standard Any UK Business Can Audit Against
Cloudswitched Team27 September 2026
Ofcom published its first assessment of AI’s impact on telecoms customers on 25 September 2026, and buried in a research document is the clearest statement a UK regulator has yet made about what acceptable AI customer service looks like. Two expectations, both short enough to quote in full. Providers must be transparent with customers when they are interacting with a GenAI application. And providers’ AI applications must not act as “gatekeepers” creating barriers to accessing support through channels providers are required to offer.
That is worth more attention than the headline figure it arrived with. Most guidance on AI governance is a page of principles that no one can be measured against. These two are close to binary: either you disclose that a customer is talking to a machine, or you do not; either the automation can block the route to a human, or it cannot. They are auditable in an afternoon. And while Ofcom regulates telecoms, the research itself shows customers are meeting AI chatbots most often in retail, where 11% of UK adult internet users encountered one — nearly double the 6% who met one at a telecoms provider. Expectations articulated in one regulated sector do not stay there. They become the benchmark that procurement questionnaires, client contracts and customers themselves apply everywhere, which makes this a useful specification for any UK business building or buying AI–powered support, regulated or not.

What Ofcom found, and what it decided to do about it
The adoption picture is one of breadth without depth. 53% of online UK adults have used GenAI tools in the past 12 months, which is a majority and a striking number for a technology this young. But that figure measures people choosing to use a tool. When you ask instead about encountering AI deployed by a business, the numbers drop sharply: 23% of UK adult internet users say they interacted with an AI chatbot in the same period. Broken down by sector, that was most commonly retail at 11%, then financial services and utilities at 8% each, and telecoms at 6%.
For telecoms specifically, 8% — the one in twelve of the headline — had interacted with an AI service in relation to their broadband, mobile or phone account. The breakdown of what they were trying to do is the most useful part of the whole dataset: 6% to resolve an issue, 3% for a price or product comparison, and 2% to make a complaint. Those figures can overlap, so they do not sum. Note the shape: the dominant use is problem–solving, and a meaningful slice is complaints — which is precisely the category where automation performs worst and where a gatekeeping failure does the most damage.
Running the other way is the 46% who report no interaction with AI at all over twelve months. Ofcom expects that to fall as adoption continues, and it almost certainly will. But it is the operative number for anyone designing a support journey today, because it describes customers who will encounter your assistant with no prior mental model of what it can do, no learned habits for getting past it, and no basis for calibrating how much to trust it. A design that assumes familiarity is designing for the other half.
On sentiment, the research is more interesting than a simple satisfaction score. Ofcom found views are mixed: some customers welcome the speed, while others read AI as a way of reducing access to human support — and critically, they read it that way “often regardless of whether AI actually resolves the issue faster”. Most people recognise the time AI could save, but “share concerns about risks to their personal data and privacy”. And many feel that negotiating with a human still gets them a better deal.
Ofcom’s regulatory conclusion, for now, is restraint. Policy Director Cristina Luna–Esteban said the regulator is “working with telecoms firms to ensure a future where AI works in consumers’ best interests and firms ensure human support remains available when they need it”. Ofcom says there is currently only “limited evidence” of AI being a systemic cause of harm, and considers existing rules sufficient — while stating it will act if evidence of harm emerges. No new rulebook, then. A stated standard and a watching brief.
“Existing rules are sufficient for now” is a conditional, not a clearance
It would be easy to read Ofcom’s finding of only limited evidence of systemic harm as permission to proceed as planned. Read the whole sentence. The regulator has published the standard it expects, said the current rules can carry it, and stated plainly that it will act if evidence of harm emerges. That is a structure with a trigger in it, and the trigger is evidence — which accumulates from customer complaints, consumer group research and press coverage rather than from regulatory inspection. A provider whose AI is quietly blocking access to human support is not in a safe position because no rule names it; it is contributing to the evidence base that would justify a rule. And for businesses outside telecoms there is a second–order effect worth noting: once a regulator has articulated a two–sentence test in public, that test gets applied by clients, insurers, procurement teams and journalists to organisations the regulator has no jurisdiction over at all.
How UK AI customer service expectations took shape this month
Ofcom’s assessment did not land in isolation. It arrived days after a consumer charity made the same argument from the opposite direction, and the two together are what make this month a turning point in the UK conversation.
The past 12 months — adoption becomes mainstream but uneven
Over the period Ofcom surveyed, 53% of online UK adults use GenAI tools on their own initiative, while 46% have no interaction with AI at all. Both facts are true of the same population at the same time, which is the defining feature of this market: a technology that is simultaneously mainstream and unfamiliar depending on which customer you are serving.
The past 12 months — businesses deploy, retail leads
23% of UK adult internet users interact with a business AI chatbot. Retail is where most people meet one, at 11%, ahead of financial services and utilities at 8% each and telecoms at 6%. The sector Ofcom regulates is therefore not the sector where customers are having most of these experiences — a point that matters for how far its expectations will travel.
July 2026 — the consumer side is polled
Separate polling on behalf of Citizens Advice finds that only 20% of UK adults consider chatbots their preferred way to communicate with essential service providers, with phone, email and instant messaging all ranking higher. The direction of travel in public sentiment is established well before either organisation publishes.
21 September 2026 — Citizens Advice makes the harm case
The charity calls on essential service providers to guarantee a “right to talk to a human”, reporting that 14% of people who struggled to reach one gave up on their issue entirely, and that some customers concluded chatbots seemed rigged to make them give up. We covered this in detail four days ago; it is the demand–side case for the same standard Ofcom would articulate.
25 September 2026 — Ofcom publishes its assessment
The regulator’s initial assessment of AI’s impact on telecoms customers sets out two expectations: transparency when a customer is interacting with a GenAI application, and no gatekeeping that creates barriers to support channels providers are required to offer. It is the supply–side specification for what Citizens Advice asked for on the demand side.
25 September 2026 — the regulatory posture is set
Ofcom concludes there is only “limited evidence” of AI being a systemic cause of harm and that existing rules are sufficient for now, while saying it will act if evidence of harm emerges. Cristina Luna–Esteban frames the work as ensuring “a future where AI works in consumers’ best interests and firms ensure human support remains available when they need it”.
27 September 2026 — what businesses now have
No new obligation for anyone outside telecoms, and no new rulebook even inside it. What exists is a published, quotable, testable standard, endorsed in substance by a consumer charity and a regulator in the same week. Any UK business can measure its own support journey against it today, and increasingly its clients will.
Ahead — the evidence question
Ofcom’s position turns on whether evidence of harm accumulates. That evidence comes from complaints, consumer research and press attention rather than inspection, which means the sector’s own behaviour over the next year determines whether the current light touch persists or is replaced with something prescriptive.
Read together, the two publications are unusually well matched. Citizens Advice supplied the harm evidence — the people who gave up, the sense of being deliberately obstructed. Ofcom supplied the specification — transparency and no gatekeeping. That combination is how UK regulation typically develops: a credible account of consumer detriment, followed by a stated expectation, followed either by industry adjustment or by rules. Businesses currently sit in the window where adjustment is voluntary and cheap.

The gap between the first bar and the third is the most commercially useful thing in this dataset. A majority of online adults have chosen to use a GenAI tool, but fewer than a quarter have met one working for a business. That is not a contradiction; it is a statement about who currently controls the interaction. When a customer opens ChatGPT they are using a tool for their own purposes and can abandon it costlessly. When they meet your assistant they are inside a process you designed, pursuing an outcome only you can deliver. The same underlying technology, encountered in those two contexts, produces entirely different expectations — and a business reasoning from the 53% to conclude “customers are comfortable with AI now” is reading the wrong number.
The sector ordering carries a second lesson. Retail at 11% is where most of these encounters happen, and retail is also where the stakes per interaction are typically lowest — a delivery query, a returns question, a stock check. Telecoms at 6% is lower but the interactions are weightier: contract disputes, service faults, billing. Customers are therefore forming their general impression of business AI largely in a low–stakes context and then carrying that impression into high–stakes ones. Whether that helps or hurts depends entirely on which experience came first.
One in twelve — and why a small number is the interesting one
The figure Ofcom led with, and the one worth planning around, is deliberately modest.
Roughly one in twelve UK adult internet users have interacted with an AI service in relation to their telecoms account in the past 12 months — 6% to resolve an issue, 3% for a price or product comparison, and 2% to make a complaint. The figures overlap, so they do not sum
A small arc is the honest visual. This is an early–stage behaviour, not a settled one, and that has a direct implication for how much weight to put on current satisfaction data: the population with an opinion about telecoms AI is still a minority, and their experiences were formed on first–generation deployments. Sentiment measured now is measuring the industry’s first attempt.
The internal breakdown is where the design guidance sits. 6% came to resolve an issue and 2% came to make a complaint. Both of those are, in the taxonomy that matters, judgement cases rather than lookups — something has gone wrong, the situation may not match a standard path, and the customer needs someone able to make a decision. Only the 3% doing price or product comparison is a genuinely good fit for automation, and even that is contested, because Ofcom also found that many customers feel negotiating with a human still gets them a better deal.
That last finding deserves isolating, because it explains something that otherwise looks irrational. Ofcom reports that customers who see AI as reducing access to human support often feel that way “regardless of whether AI actually resolves the issue faster”. Speed, in other words, does not buy trust. The reason is that the customer’s objection is not about latency but about whose interests the system serves. A customer who suspects the assistant exists to protect the provider’s margin rather than to solve their problem will not be reassured by it protecting that margin more efficiently. Combine this with the finding that people believe human negotiation gets a better deal, and the picture is coherent: some customers read automation as the company withdrawing its discretion, and discretion is the thing they were hoping to appeal to.
The practical consequence for any business is uncomfortable but clarifying. You cannot resolve this trust deficit by improving the model. A faster, more accurate assistant that still cannot make a concession, waive a charge or exercise judgement will be experienced as a better–built wall. What changes the perception is evidence that the human route remains genuinely open — which is exactly what Ofcom’s second expectation requires, and why the gatekeeping test is the more substantive of the two.

The fifth row is the transparency question most businesses get wrong without meaning to. Giving an assistant a friendly first name is a reasonable design choice for warmth, and it becomes a transparency problem the moment a customer reasonably concludes they are speaking to a member of staff called Alex. Ofcom’s expectation is that it must be transparent when a customer is interacting with a GenAI application — and a persona that obscures that, even unintentionally, works against it. The fix is not to remove the personality but to state the position once, plainly, at the start.
The seventh row connects to a finding businesses tend to underrate. Ofcom reports that most people recognise the time AI could save but “share concerns about risks to their personal data and privacy”. Those concerns are not satisfied by a privacy policy, because nobody reads one mid–conversation. They are addressed by a sentence at the point of interaction explaining what happens to what the customer types — and, on the business side, by having actually established the answer: whether the assistant is a third–party service, what it retains, and whether content is used for model training. Support conversations are unusually rich in personal data, so under UK GDPR those are questions with real answers rather than rhetorical ones.

The two–test audit — ten minutes, and the answers are binary
Ofcom’s expectations are unusually easy to check because neither is a matter of degree. Test one, transparency: open your own support journey as a customer and ask whether, within the first exchange, you are told plainly that you are dealing with AI rather than a person. Not implied by tone, not disclosed in a footer, not recoverable from a terms page — stated. If your assistant has a human first name and no disclosure line, you have failed this test in a way your customers will notice before you do. Test two, gatekeeping: ask for a human. If you get one immediately, you pass. If you get another attempt to help, a request to describe the problem first, or a suggestion to try the help centre, you do not — and note that failing this test is usually a deliberate configuration choice made by somebody optimising a metric rather than an oversight. Then apply both tests specifically to the complaints route, because that is where 2% of Ofcom’s telecoms respondents were heading and where a gate does the most damage. Write down what you find and date it. That document is the beginning of a defensible position.
Why this belongs on a board agenda rather than a support team’s backlog
The instinct is to treat AI customer service as an operational matter for whoever runs support. This month’s two publications are an argument for handling it a level higher, for a reason that has nothing to do with technology. The decisions that produce the failures — what to measure, whether escalation is unconditional, whether complaints may be automated, what the assistant is allowed to concede — are all trade–offs between cost and customer access. Those are not engineering choices and they should not be made by whoever is closest to the tooling. They are the kind of judgement a virtual CIO engagement exists to surface: decisions with a clear short–term financial upside and a diffuse, delayed reputational cost.
There is a supplier dimension too, and it is arriving faster than most businesses expect. Ofcom’s expectations apply to telecoms providers, but the organisations reading them include every regulated firm wondering whether its own regulator will say something similar — and those firms pass their obligations down through procurement. A written statement of how your business uses AI in customer–facing processes, what it discloses, and how a customer reaches a human is becoming a routine due diligence question rather than an unusual one. Having the answer drafted is considerably cheaper than assembling it during a tender.
The security and data protection angle rounds it out, and it is where the Cyber Essentials discipline of enumeration earns its keep again. An AI support assistant is a third–party service processing your customers’ personal data, reachable from the internet, integrated with your systems, and frequently procured outside the IT function by a marketing or operations team. That makes it precisely the kind of asset that never reaches an inventory, never gets a data protection assessment and never appears in a scope conversation. Ofcom’s finding that customers “share concerns about risks to their personal data and privacy” is, for once, a customer instinct that matches the internal reality rather well.
The final point is about timing, and it is the reason this is worth acting on in a quiet week rather than a busy one. Right now the standard is published, the obligation is absent, and the fixes are a disclosure line and an escalation path. If evidence of harm accumulates and Ofcom or another regulator moves to rules, the same changes will have to be made anyway — to a deadline, with documentation requirements, and alongside every competitor doing the same thing at the same time. The work does not get smaller by waiting. Only the discretion about how to do it does.

Frequently asked questions
Does Ofcom’s assessment create new obligations for our business?
Not directly, and not at all if you are outside telecoms. Ofcom concluded there is only “limited evidence” of AI being a systemic cause of harm and that existing rules are sufficient for now, so no new rulebook has been issued even for the providers it regulates. What it has published is a clear statement of what it expects: transparency when customers are dealing with GenAI, and no gatekeeping that blocks access to support channels providers are required to offer. For everyone else that is not a legal requirement but a published benchmark — and published benchmarks tend to migrate into client contracts, procurement questionnaires and press scrutiny well ahead of any regulation.
What does “gatekeeper” actually mean in practice?
It means the automation standing between a customer and a channel they are entitled to use. The clearest failure is an assistant that will only hand over to a person when it concludes it cannot help — because that is precisely the judgement it is least able to make when it has misunderstood the problem. Softer versions count too: requiring a customer to describe their issue to the bot before a phone number is revealed, removing contact details from a contact page so the widget is the only entry point, or routing complaints through the same automated front door as routine queries. The test is simple. If a customer asks for a human, do they get one immediately and unconditionally?
How much disclosure counts as transparent?
One plain sentence at the start of the interaction, stating that the customer is dealing with an AI assistant rather than a person. Not implied by tone, not disclosed in a footer, not recoverable from a terms page. The common trap is an assistant given a human first name for warmth — a reasonable design choice that becomes a transparency problem the moment a customer reasonably believes they are speaking to a member of staff. You do not have to strip out the personality to fix this; you have to state the position once, clearly, before the conversation begins. It is the cheapest of all the changes discussed here and the one most often left undone.
If only 8% of people use AI for telecoms, is this really urgent?
The 8% is a reason to act now rather than a reason to wait. It tells you this behaviour is early–stage, which means current sentiment is being formed on the industry’s first attempts and habits are not yet set. Ofcom expects the 46% who have had no AI interaction at all to shrink. Designing the disclosure and escalation properly while your volumes are small is straightforward; retrofitting it once automation is load–bearing across your whole support operation is a project. The businesses that will struggle are those that scale a deployment built on a deflection assumption and then have to unpick it under external pressure.
Why does making our chatbot faster not improve satisfaction?
Because the objection is not about speed. Ofcom found that customers who see AI as reducing access to human support often feel that way “regardless of whether AI actually resolves the issue faster”, and separately that many believe negotiating with a human gets them a better deal. Put those together and the complaint becomes legible: some customers read automation as the company withdrawing its discretion, and discretion is what they were hoping to appeal to. A faster assistant that still cannot waive a charge, make a concession or exercise judgement is experienced as a better–built wall. What shifts perception is visible evidence that the human route is genuinely open.
We are not in telecoms. Why should we care what Ofcom thinks?
Two reasons. First, Ofcom’s own data shows customers are meeting AI chatbots most often in retail, at 11%, nearly double the 6% who meet one at a telecoms provider — so the experiences shaping public expectations are largely happening outside the regulated sector. Second, a two–sentence test published by a regulator becomes the reference point everyone else uses. Clients writing supplier questionnaires, insurers assessing conduct risk, regulated firms pushing obligations down their supply chain and journalists writing the next story all now have a standard to measure against, and none of them need jurisdiction over you to apply it.
What is the single cheapest thing to change this week?
Two changes, both usually achievable in days. Add a plain sentence at the start of every AI interaction stating that the customer is dealing with an assistant rather than a person. And make the route to a human unconditional — a visible phone number or email address reachable without engaging the bot, plus a handover that fires whenever someone asks for a person rather than only when the system decides it cannot help. Then apply both specifically to your complaints route, since 2% of Ofcom’s telecoms respondents were using AI to make a complaint and that is where a barrier causes the most damage. Nothing on that list requires a better model or a new platform.
How should we handle the privacy concerns Ofcom identified?
Start by establishing the facts internally, because you cannot explain a position you have not determined. If the assistant is a third–party service, that provider is processing customer messages — which in support contexts routinely contain names, account details and sometimes financial or health circumstances volunteered while explaining a problem. Under UK GDPR you need to know the lawful basis, what the processor terms say, how long content is retained and whether it is used for model training. Reputable providers answer all of that clearly. Then surface a short version at the point of interaction, because Ofcom’s finding is about customer concern, and a privacy policy nobody reads mid–conversation does not address concern.
Who in our business should own this?
Someone more senior than the person who configures the tool. The decisions that produce the failures in this research are not technical: what to measure, whether escalation is unconditional, whether complaints may be automated, and what the assistant is permitted to concede. Each is a trade–off between cost and customer access, with a clear short–term financial upside and a diffuse, delayed reputational cost — which is exactly the shape of decision that gets made badly when it is delegated to whoever is closest to the tooling. It also needs someone who will notice that an AI assistant is a third–party service processing personal data and therefore belongs in your asset inventory.
Is regulation likely to follow?
Ofcom has left itself the option in explicit terms: existing rules are sufficient “for now”, and it will act if evidence of harm emerges. The important detail is where that evidence comes from. Not inspection, but complaints, consumer group research and press coverage — which means the sector’s own conduct over the next year determines the outcome. Given that Citizens Advice published substantial harm evidence four days before Ofcom’s assessment, the accumulation has arguably already started. Businesses that adopt the standard voluntarily now are protecting the light–touch regime they currently benefit from, as well as doing the work they would have to do anyway under a deadline later.
Adopt the standard while it is still voluntary
The changes Ofcom’s expectations imply — a disclosure line, an unconditional escalation path, complaints kept out of automation, a documented data position — are the same changes a business would have to make if rules arrived. The only thing that shrinks by waiting is your discretion over how to do them.
